How White-Label Pricing Works: Wholesale Rates, Retail Rates and Your Margin

Factor42 MediaNov 1, 20266 min read

Most agencies that look into white-label fulfillment ask the same question first: what does it cost? The honest answer is that the partner's price is only half of it. How white-label pricing works matters more than any single number, because the model decides whether the service makes your agency money or quietly loses it.

This guide explains the wholesale-and-retail model behind white-label digital marketing, how to set a retail price your clients will accept, where your margin actually comes from, and what a partner's rate card should tell you.

How does white-label pricing work?

White-label pricing works on a wholesale and retail model: your partner charges your agency a wholesale rate to do the work, you charge your client a retail rate for the service, and the difference is your margin. The client only ever sees your retail price, because the service is sold under your brand.

There are three numbers in every white-label arrangement:

  • The retail rate: what your client pays your agency for the service.
  • The wholesale rate: what your agency pays the fulfillment partner.
  • The ad spend: the media budget that goes to Google, Meta or another platform.
  • The first two are yours to manage. The third belongs to the client's campaign, and the cleanest arrangements keep it separate from both fees. More on that below.

What is a wholesale fulfillment rate?

A wholesale fulfillment rate is the price a white-label partner charges an agency to deliver the work, set below what the agency will charge its own client. It covers the hands-on production: campaign builds, trafficking, quality checks, optimization and branded reporting.

Partners price that rate in a few common ways:

  • Flat monthly fee per account or campaign, often tiered by channel.
  • A percentage of the client's ad spend, usually with a monthly minimum.
  • Per campaign or per flight, common for programmatic, streaming TV and out-of-home.
  • Hourly or retainer blocks, more common for strategy or creative than for fulfillment.

Factor42 prices its wholesale rate by channel mix and volume: the more channels and campaigns an agency sends, the more the rate reflects that scale. The structure matters as much as the level. A flat fee is predictable for you. A percentage of spend scales with the account, which is fair on large budgets and expensive on small ones. Ask which model a partner uses for each channel before you compare numbers.

How should an agency set its retail price?

An agency should set its retail price from the value to the client and the local market, then check it against the wholesale rate, not the other way around. Starting from the wholesale rate and adding a fixed markup is the most common way agencies underprice.

A practical order of work:

  • Find what clients in your market already pay for the service, from your own proposals and competitors' published packages.
  • Decide what your agency adds on top of fulfillment: strategy, account management, reporting reviews, the client relationship.
  • Set a retail price that reflects that full service.
  • Confirm the gap to the wholesale rate covers your account management time and leaves profit.

If the gap is too thin after that check, the answer is usually to change the package, not to cut the retail price. Bundling channels, setting a minimum ad spend per client, or moving small accounts to a simpler tier all protect margin better than discounting.

Where does the margin come from?

The margin comes from the difference between your retail rate and the wholesale rate, minus the time your own team spends on the account. That last part is the one agencies forget, and it is where most white-label services lose money.

A simple example, with made-up numbers to show the math rather than anyone's real rates: an agency charges a client 2,000 dollars a month to manage paid search. The fulfillment partner's wholesale rate for that account is 1,000 dollars. The gross difference is 1,000 dollars. If the agency's account manager spends six hours a month on calls, reviews and reporting for that client, and those hours cost the agency 50 dollars each, the real margin is 700 dollars, or 35% of the retail price.

The same math explains why white-label fulfillment is attractive in the first place. Doing that work in-house means paying a specialist's salary, tools and training whether or not the accounts exist. With a wholesale rate, the cost of delivery only appears when there is a client paying for it.

What is the difference between margin and markup?

Margin is profit as a share of the retail price, while markup is profit as a share of the wholesale cost. They describe the same dollars differently, and mixing them up leads to underpricing.

In the example above, the 1,000 dollar gross difference is a 50% margin on the 2,000 dollar retail price, but a 100% markup on the 1,000 dollar wholesale rate. An agency that aims for "50% markup" thinking it means "50% margin" would charge 1,500 dollars instead of 2,000, and keep less than a third as much profit after its own time (200 dollars instead of 700). Pick one measure, use it in every pricing conversation, and write it down.

How should ad spend be handled?

Ad spend should be kept separate from both the retail and wholesale fees, ideally paid directly from the client's card or billing on an account the client or your agency owns. That keeps your fee transparent and keeps the client's media budget from being mistaken for your revenue.

Separating spend has three practical benefits:

  • Clients can see exactly what reached the platform, which builds trust at renewal time.
  • Your margin stays tied to the service, not to how much the client spends that month.
  • If you ever change partners, the ad accounts and their history stay where they are.

Some agencies add a percentage on top of ad spend as part of their own retail pricing. That is a legitimate choice, but it should be stated in the client agreement rather than buried in a media invoice.

What should be in a rate card?

A white-label rate card should show the wholesale price for each channel, what is included at that price, and what costs extra, so you can build your own client packages without guessing. A rate card that only lists a number per channel is not enough.

Look for:

  • Price per channel or campaign type, and the pricing model behind each one.
  • Minimums: minimum ad spend, minimum term or minimum number of accounts.
  • What is included: builds, optimization cadence, reporting format and schedule.
  • What costs extra: creative production, landing pages, rush launches, extra reports.
  • Volume tiers: how the rate changes as you send more accounts or channels.
  • Terms: notice period, and what happens to accounts and data if you leave.

Common white-label pricing mistakes

The most common mistakes are pricing from the wholesale rate up, ignoring your own team's time, mixing ad spend into fees, and competing on price instead of service. Each one is easy to avoid once you see it.

It also helps to agree the scope of work before agreeing a price, so you are comparing like with like. Our guide to what white-label PPC management should include is a useful checklist for that conversation, and how white-label PPC works for agencies covers the handoff between your team and a partner.

The short version

White-label pricing is simple on paper: retail rate minus wholesale rate, minus your own time, equals margin. The agencies that make it work set retail prices from client value, keep ad spend separate, and choose a partner whose rate card is clear enough to package around.
