Blog/Choosing a partner

"Full-service" is one of the most reassuring phrases in marketing, and one of the most slippery. It suggests a provider that handles everything, so you don't have to worry about the gaps. But full-service means very different things to different providers, and what's included is often narrower than the name implies — with the rest showing up as extras, add-ons, or things you discover you still have to handle yourself. Knowing what full-service *should* mean helps you tell real comprehensiveness from a comforting label. Here's the standard to hold providers to.

Factor42 Media4 min read

Why "full-service" is so slippery

The problem with "full-service" is that there's no fixed definition, so providers apply it generously. A provider might call themselves full-service while really excelling at one or two channels and treating the rest as afterthoughts or extras. The phrase does reassuring work in a sales conversation — implying you can hand over everything and relax — while the actual scope underneath may have significant gaps.

The result is that businesses sign up expecting comprehensive coverage and later discover the boundaries: the channels not really included, the services that cost extra, the coordination that still falls on them. The label promised full; the reality delivered partial. Knowing what genuinely full-service should include lets you check the reassuring word against the actual substance.

What full-service should actually include

Real full-service marketing should cover the whole job of getting your business in front of customers — not just a slice of it. At minimum, that means:

All the channels you need, not just one or two. Genuine full-service covers the range of channels relevant to your business — search, social, streaming, email, and more — rather than doing one well and gesturing at the rest. If you still need other providers to cover important channels, it wasn't full-service.

Strategy, not just execution. Full-service should include planning what to do, not just doing what you tell it. A provider that only executes tasks you hand them isn't providing the full service of figuring out the right approach.

Coordination across everything. Truly comprehensive marketing coordinates the channels so they work together — not just runs them separately. Coordination is part of the job, and its absence is a common hidden gap.

Clear measurement of the whole. Full-service should include showing you how everything is performing together, in one clear view, so you can see the whole result — not fragmented reports per channel.

Ongoing management and adjustment. The full service includes actively managing and improving your marketing over time, not setting it up and leaving it.

If a provider genuinely delivers all of this, "full-service" is earned. If they deliver only some of it, the label is doing more work than the service.

What "full-service" usually leaves out

In practice, the common gaps between the label and the reality are:

  • Channels treated as afterthoughts or extras — a provider strong in one or two areas that half-covers or up-charges for the rest.
  • Coordination that doesn't happen — channels run separately even under one provider, so they don't actually work together.
  • Strategy that's really just execution — a provider who does what you say but doesn't bring the thinking.
  • Fragmented reporting — per-channel numbers rather than a clear whole-picture view.
  • Things that cost extra — services you assumed were included turning out to be add-ons.
  • These gaps are exactly where "full-service" tends to fall short of its promise, and where you should probe before signing.

Full-service and consolidation

Here's the connection: what full-service should mean — all your channels, coordinated, planned, measured as a whole, and actively managed — is essentially the definition of consolidated marketing. Genuine full-service and true consolidation are the same idea: your entire marketing run comprehensively and coordinately as one, rather than partially and in pieces.

That's why the providers who deliver on the full-service promise are the ones who genuinely run your marketing as a unified whole from one place — because only that structure can actually cover everything, coordinate it, and account for the total result. A provider claiming full-service while running a fragmented or single-channel operation can't deliver the real thing, no matter the label. If you want what full-service promises, look for genuine consolidation behind it.

Hold the label to the standard

"Full-service" should mean your entire marketing handled comprehensively — all channels, strategy, coordination, whole-picture measurement, and ongoing management — but it often means considerably less. Hold any provider claiming the label to the real standard, and check whether the substance matches the reassuring word.
