Blog/Choosing a partner

A marketing funnel is just a practical way of thinking about how strangers become customers — and how to guide them through that process deliberately rather than leaving it to chance. The "funnel" image captures a simple truth: many people enter at the top as casual prospects, and a smaller number make it through to the bottom as buyers. A well-built funnel maximizes how many complete that journey. Here's how to build one that actually converts, for a small business.

Factor42 Media4 min read

What a funnel actually is

Picture a funnel: wide at the top, narrow at the bottom. At the top, a broad group of people become aware of your business. As they move down, some grow interested, some seriously consider you, and some become customers. Naturally, fewer people reach each stage than the one before — not everyone who hears of you will buy — which is why it's shaped like a funnel rather than a pipe.

The purpose of thinking in funnel terms is to be deliberate about each stage: attracting enough of the right people at the top, and then guiding as many as possible down through interest and consideration to a purchase. A funnel that converts well is one where people don't leak out along the way for want of the right nudge at the right moment.

The stages and what each needs

Building a funnel that converts means addressing each stage with the right approach:

Top — Attract. Draw in the right people and build awareness. This stage needs channels that reach broad, relevant audiences and make strong first impressions — getting your business in front of potential customers who don't yet know you.

Middle — Engage. Turn awareness into genuine interest and keep people moving. This stage needs ways to engage prospects, provide value, and stay present — nurturing the attention you've captured so it doesn't fade.

Lower middle — Persuade. Help people considering you decide in your favor. This stage needs reassurance and reinforcement — reviews, targeted follow-up, presence when they're comparing options.

Bottom — Convert. Capture people at the moment they're ready to act. This stage needs to meet buying intent directly and make it easy to become a customer.

A funnel that converts well tends every stage, not just one. Attract without converting and you get attention but no sales; try to convert without attracting and there's no one in the funnel to convert.

Why most small business funnels leak

The most common funnel problem for small businesses is leakage — people entering but slipping out before they buy, because some stage isn't being tended. And the usual reason a stage goes untended is that the business is only doing part of the funnel.

A business relying on one or two channels typically covers one or two stages and neglects the rest. Only running search? You capture some ready buyers but never fill the top of the funnel with new awareness, so the funnel starves. Only running social? You may build awareness but fail to capture people at the decision point, so they leak out the bottom. The uncovered stages are where prospects escape. A leaky funnel isn't usually a sign of bad marketing at any one stage — it's a sign of missing stages.

Plugging the leaks means covering the whole funnel, with each stage tended and — crucially — connected to the next, so people are actively guided down rather than left to drift out.

Why a converting funnel requires coordination

Here's the key: a funnel that converts isn't just a set of stages covered independently — it's stages that hand off to each other, moving people down. The awareness you build at the top must feed the engagement in the middle, which must feed the persuasion below, which must feed the conversion at the bottom. Each stage passes people to the next. That hand-off is what makes it a funnel rather than four disconnected activities.

Those hand-offs require your channels and stages to work together as one coordinated effort — which is exactly what running your marketing as a single operation provides and what fragmentation prevents. When separate vendors run separate channels with no coordination, there are no hand-offs; each operates in isolation, and your funnel is really just disconnected pieces with people leaking out between them. When one team runs everything together, the stages connect into a genuine funnel that carries people from stranger to customer. Building a funnel that converts, in other words, is largely about running your marketing as a coordinated whole.

Build a funnel that works

A marketing funnel that converts attracts the right people at the top and deliberately guides as many as possible down through interest, consideration, and decision — with every stage tended and connected to the next. Cover the whole funnel in a coordinated way, and you turn more strangers into customers instead of watching them leak out along the way.
